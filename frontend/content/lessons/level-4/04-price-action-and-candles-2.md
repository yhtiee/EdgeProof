Some candle shapes have names, and books often attach meanings to them [@nison-candlesticks]. Three you'll see everywhere:

- **Engulfing:** a candle whose body completely covers the previous candle's body, in the opposite direction.
- **Pin bar** (or hammer): a small body with one long wick, showing price was pushed far one way and rejected.
- **Doji:** open and close almost equal, a sign of indecision.

---

On their own, these shapes appear constantly, in every direction, on every timeframe. A pin bar in the middle of nowhere means very little.

What people find useful, if anything, is the pattern in **context**: for example, a pin bar that rejects a tested [[support]] zone, in the direction of the [[trend]].

---

Patterns also need exact definitions to be tested. "A long wick" could mean anything. "A lower wick at least twice the body, and the close in the top third of the candle" can be checked by anyone, on any chart.

---

::question{type="mc"}
Which description of a pin bar can be tested?
- [ ] "A candle that shows strong rejection"
- [ ] "A candle with a long wick that looks bullish"
- [x] "A candle whose lower wick is at least twice its body, closing in the top third of its range"
? Only the third gives the same answer for everyone. Words like "strong" and "looks" can't be tested.
::

---

::question{type="mc"}
You've read that bullish engulfing candles "signal reversals". What's the evidence-based next step?
- [ ] Trade every one you see
- [x] Define it exactly, add context rules, and test it on data before trusting it
- [ ] Ignore it, because patterns never work
? A pattern is an idea to test, not a fact. Testing tells you whether it adds anything after costs in the context you trade.
::

---

::takeaway
Candle patterns are ideas, not signals. They mean little alone; define them exactly, add context, and test them before relying on one.
::
