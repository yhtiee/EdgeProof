In Level 4 you saw that a higher [[timeframe]] gives context to a lower one. Alexander Elder turned that into a structured method he called the triple screen [@elder-trading-for-a-living].

---

The idea uses three timeframes, each roughly five times longer than the next:

1. **The long timeframe** sets the direction. For example, the weekly chart decides whether you only look for buys or only for sells.
2. **The middle timeframe** finds the setup. For example, on the daily chart you wait for a pullback against that direction.
3. **The short timeframe** times the entry. For example, on the 4-hour chart you enter when the pullback shows signs of ending.

---

The factor of five is a guide, not a law. Daily, 4-hour and 1-hour charts, or 4-hour, 1-hour and 15-minute charts, follow the same spirit.

The point is a clear chain: direction from the top, setup in the middle, timing at the bottom, with each timeframe answering only its own question.

---

::question{type="mc"}
In a triple screen setup, the weekly trend is down. On the daily chart, price rallies up into resistance. What would the method look for?
- [ ] A buy, because the daily chart is rising
- [x] A sell, once the short timeframe shows the rally ending
- [ ] Nothing, because the timeframes disagree
? The long timeframe sets the direction: sells only. A rally on the middle timeframe is the setup, and the short timeframe times the entry.
::

---

::takeaway
The triple screen uses three timeframes about five times apart: the longest for direction, the middle for the setup, the shortest for entry timing.
::
