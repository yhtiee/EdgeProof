When a number is released, the market compares it with the forecast. The gap between actual and expected is the surprise, and the surprise is what tends to move prices.

---

A strong number that matches the forecast often causes little reaction. A number that's much better or worse than forecast can cause a big one. Sometimes the reaction even looks backwards: good news, but not as good as expected, can push a currency down.

Revisions to earlier releases, and other details inside the report, can matter as well, which is why first reactions often reverse.

---

::question{type="mc"}
US jobs growth was forecast at 180,000. The actual figure is 120,000, while the previous month's was revised higher. Why might the reaction be hard to predict?
- [ ] Because jobs data never moves currencies
- [x] Because the headline missed the forecast but the revision was positive, and traders weigh both
- [ ] Because 120,000 is a good number
? Markets react to the whole release against expectations. Mixed surprises can pull prices both ways within seconds.
::

::takeaway
Prices react to the surprise: actual against expected, plus revisions and details. Reactions are fast and often reverse, which makes news hard to trade and important to plan around.
::
