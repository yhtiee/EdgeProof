An [[interest rate]] is the price of money: what a bank pays you to keep your savings, or charges you to borrow. Each currency has its own rates, set mainly by its [[central bank]].

---

Big investors move money around the world looking for the best return for the risk. If one country's rates rise, savings and bonds in its currency pay more. Investors need that currency to buy them, so demand for it can rise, and its price with it.

The reverse happens too: falling rates can make a currency less attractive.

---

Here's the twist that confuses beginners: markets move on **expectations**, not just on decisions. If everyone expects a rate rise next month, prices often move before it happens. When the rise finally comes, the currency may barely react, because it was already expected.

What moves prices most is a surprise: a decision, or a change in outlook, that markets didn't expect.

---

::question{type="mc"}
A central bank raises rates by 0.25%, exactly as everyone expected. Why might its currency hardly move?
- [ ] Rate changes never affect currencies
- [x] The rise was already expected, so it was already reflected in the price

? Markets price in what they expect. An expected decision brings little new information; a surprise would.

---

::takeaway
Higher interest rates can attract money to a currency, and lower rates can push it away. But prices move mostly on surprises and changing expectations, not on the decision itself.
::
