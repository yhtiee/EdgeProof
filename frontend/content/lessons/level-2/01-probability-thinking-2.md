Most beginners judge each trade on its own: a win feels like being right, a loss like being wrong. That's the wrong way to measure.

Mark Douglas described it as thinking in probabilities: anything can happen on any one trade, and an [[edge]] is only a higher chance of one outcome over another [@douglas-trading-in-the-zone].

---

A useful habit: think in batches of trades, say 20 at a time. Inside a batch, you can't know which trades will win. You can only know whether you followed your rules on each one.

Over many batches, a real edge shows up in the totals, not in any single trade.

---

This separates two things people mix up:

- A **good decision** follows your tested rules, with the planned risk.
- A **good outcome** is a winning trade.

You can make a good decision and lose, or a bad decision and win. Only the decisions are in your control.

---

::question{type="mc"}
A trader breaks their rules, doubles their size, and wins. How should that trade be judged?
- [ ] As a good trade, because it made money
- [x] As a bad decision with a lucky outcome
- [ ] As proof that the rules should change
? The result was luck. Repeating that decision, oversized and outside the rules, will eventually produce a large loss.
::

---

::takeaway
Judge your decisions, not single outcomes. Any one trade can win or lose; only a long series of rule-following trades shows whether you have an edge.
::
