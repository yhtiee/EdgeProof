Market participants commonly group each [[currency pair]] into categories based on overall trading activity [@babypips-pipsology].

These categories help traders understand how easily a currency can be bought or sold and what transaction costs to expect.

---

Major pairs join the US dollar with another heavily traded global currency. Examples include EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, and USD/CHF.

In these combinations, the US dollar serves as either the [[base currency]] or the [[quote currency]]. Because the US dollar dominates global commerce, major pairs represent the largest share of daily market turnover.

---

Minor pairs, also known as cross currency pairs, link two major currencies without including the US dollar [@babypips-pipsology].

Common examples of minor pairs include EUR/GBP, AUD/JPY, and GBP/CHF. These pairs allow direct transactions between strong global economies without exchanging through the US dollar first.

---

Because major pairs attract large trading volume, they offer deep market liquidity. As a result, a [[broker]] typically charges a narrower [[spread]] on major pairs than on cross pairs [@babypips-pipsology].

Minor pairs often see lower volume. This reduced trading activity can mean wider spreads and sudden price moves of several [[pip|pips]] at once.

::takeaway
Major pairs join the US dollar with another heavily traded currency, while minor pairs link two major currencies without the US dollar. High trading volume in major pairs generally leads to lower spread costs.
