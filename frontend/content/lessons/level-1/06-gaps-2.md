For more than three years, Switzerland's [[central bank]] promised that 1 euro would never cost less than 1.20 Swiss francs. Traders treated that level like a floor.

Then, without warning, it stopped. On 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank discontinued its minimum exchange rate of 1.20 Swiss francs per euro and lowered its deposit rate to −0.75% [@snb-2015-jordan].

---

The franc jumped in value within minutes. Many banks stopped quoting prices, so there was almost no one to trade with.

Traders who held positions against the franc saw their stops fill far beyond where they'd placed them. Some lost more than everything in their account, and ended up owing their [[broker]] money.

---

One broker's experience shows the scale. After the Swiss franc move in January 2015, FXCM's clients owed it more than $225 million in negative balances [@cnbc-fxcm-2015].

Several brokers were badly hurt too. The event showed that a trader's risk depends on the broker's strength as well.

---

Rules changed afterwards. In the EU and UK, retail clients now get [[negative balance protection]]: a client cannot lose more than the money in the account [@fca-ps19-18].

That protects your account from going below zero. It doesn't protect the money you deposited, and it doesn't apply everywhere: it depends on where your broker is regulated.

::takeaway
Even a level everyone trusts can break without warning. Stops can fill far away in a gap, so size every trade as if the worst day could happen, and know whether your account has negative balance protection.
::
