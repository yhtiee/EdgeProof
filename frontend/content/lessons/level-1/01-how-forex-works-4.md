Exchange rates move when the balance between buyers and sellers for a currency shifts.

Every transaction in the forex market involves trading one currency for another. When looking at a [[currency pair]], the exchange rate represents the price of the [[base currency]] priced in the [[quote currency]]. If more buyers want a currency than sellers are willing to provide at that level, the price rises until new sellers emerge. When sellers outnumber buyers, the price falls until buyers return.

---

Currencies follow the basic rule of supply and demand.

When international demand for a nation's exports, services, or investments increases, foreign buyers must acquire that nation's currency to complete those purchases. That rising demand can lift the currency's value against other currencies. In contrast, when demand weakens or the supply of a currency circulating in the market outpaces demand, its exchange value tends to drop.

---

Central banks set their country's main interest rate and change it to steer inflation and growth [@fed-monetary-policy]. Those decisions can move currency demand.

When a central bank increases its policy rate, cash deposits and government debt in that country tend to offer higher returns. International investors often move funds toward currencies that pay higher yields, assuming they view the underlying risks as acceptable. This inflow of capital raises demand for that currency and can drive up its exchange value against lower-yielding peers.

---

Economic reports shape what investors expect central banks to do next.

Policy makers watch inflation, employment and other data when they set rates [@ecb-monetary-policy]. For instance, if inflation stays higher than a central bank's target, investors often prepare for rate increases well before any official policy vote takes place. The market adjusts prices in advance based on these expectations.

---

Exchange rates fluctuate throughout the trading week because new information arrives without pause.

Commercial banks, investment funds, and trade corporations constantly absorb economic releases, government announcements, and geopolitical shifts. Because each participant manages different requirements and risk tolerances, their collective trades continuously shift the balance of supply and demand.
